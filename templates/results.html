How to Read This Patient Care Guide
Medical imaging reports contain technical terms intended for doctors. This guide translates your scan findings into everyday language so you can actively participate in your treatment decisions with your doctor.

1. What Did Your Scan Show?

Healthy lungs are filled with air and appear dark or black on an X-ray because X-rays pass easily through air.

{% if 'Abnormal' in scan.condition_predicted or scan.confidence_score > 50 %} Your scan revealed a cloudy or dense white patch (called an opacity or consolidation) in your lower lung. This occurs when normal air in the microscopic lung sacs is temporarily replaced by fluid, mucus, or inflammatory cells. {% else %} Your scan showed clear, dark lungs without any unexpected shadows, dense fluid buildup, or abnormal white spots. {% endif %}

2. Does This Mean I Have Pneumonia?

Not by itself. An X-ray shows the physical shadow inside the lung, but a picture alone cannot tell which specific bacteria, virus, or condition caused it.

{% if 'Abnormal' in scan.condition_predicted or scan.confidence_score > 50 %} When a cloudy patch appears alongside symptoms like fever, coughing, or shortness of breath, a lung infection such as pneumonia is the most common cause. Your doctor will listen to your chest, check your oxygen, and may order blood tests to confirm pneumonia and choose the right medication. {% else %} The absence of a white shadow strongly suggests you do not have acute consolidation or typical pneumonia. Your doctor will evaluate other causes if you still have cough or discomfort. {% endif %}

3. Empowered Care: What to Ask Your Doctor Patient Checklist
  • "Does this shadow confirm pneumonia, bronchitis, or another lung condition?" Helps clarify your doctor's working diagnosis.
  • "Do I need prescription antibiotics, an inhaler, or rest and fever relievers?" Understand which treatment is appropriate for bacterial vs. viral infections.
  • "How many days should it take before my breathing and energy begin to improve?" Gives you a realistic timeline for recovery.
  • "Do I need a follow-up chest X-ray in 6 to 8 weeks?" Doctors often recommend a repeat scan after recovery to confirm the cloudiness has fully disappeared.
Critical Warning: When to Seek Immediate Emergency Medical Care
If you develop any of the following red-flag symptoms, do not wait for a scheduled appointment. Call emergency services (e.g. 911 / local emergency) or go to the nearest emergency department immediately:
• Severe shortness of breath or gasping for air
• Chest pain that intensifies sharply when breathing
• Bluish, pale, or gray tint on lips or fingernails
• Sudden confusion, dizziness, or extreme weakness
5. Medical Glossary: Decoding Words on Your Report
Airspace Opacity A cloudy or white region on an X-ray where normal lung air has been displaced by fluid, inflammation, or mucus.
Consolidation Solidification of lung tissue caused by inflammatory fluid buildup inside the tiny microscopic air sacs (alveoli).
CAD System (Computer-Aided Detection) A specialized image-processing tool that highlights dense areas to assist the radiologist during review.
PA View (Posteroanterior) The standard chest X-ray position where the beam travels from your back to front while standing upright.
